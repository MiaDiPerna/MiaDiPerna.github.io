Class notes

Important notes on going public

Why a company goes public

  • To raise a large amount of money for growth, research and computing power.
  • To let founders, employees and early investors sell their shares.
  • To have a public stock price for paying employees in stock, buying other companies and borrowing money.
  • Trade-off: public companies must report their finances every quarter and answer to many shareholders.

Who provides the money and who receives it

  • Provides: investors who buy shares in the IPO.
  • Underwriters (investment banks) buy the shares and resell them to investors, keeping a fee called the underwriting discount.
  • New (primary) shares: the money goes to the company.
  • Existing (secondary) shares: the money goes to the shareholders who sell. The company gets nothing.

The simple math: who gets the money (before fees)

  • Company gets: new shares sold × IPO price.
  • Selling stockholders get: their shares sold × IPO price.
  • Total raised: add the two together.
  • Everyone sells at the same price. The only difference is how many shares each side sells.

Anthropic's IPO by the numbers (from the prospectus)

  • Price: $83 per share. Ticker: ANT on the Nasdaq.
  • Anthropic sells 956,351,354 new shares × $83 = about $79.4 billion.
  • Selling stockholders sell 23,648,646 shares × $83 = about $1.96 billion.
  • Total: 980,000,000 shares = $81.34 billion (before fees).
  • Separate private sale: Google, NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC are buying about $26 billion more at $83 per share.

IPO underpricing (Anthropic class example)

  • IPO price: $83 per share. Opening price: $111.50 per share.
  • First-day pop: $111.50 − $83 = $28.50 per share.
  • Underpricing %: $28.50 ÷ $83 = about 34%.
  • Money left on the table: $28.50 × number of shares sold. This is money the sellers could have received but the IPO buyers got instead.
  • Anthropic: 980,000,000 shares × $28.50 = about $27.9 billion left on the table.
  • Winners: investors who got shares at $83. Losers: Anthropic and the selling stockholders, who sold for less than the market would pay.
  • Why companies accept it: it makes sure all shares sell, rewards the big investors who committed early, creates good publicity, and lowers the risk of the stock dropping on day one.

Who invests in IPOs

  • Institutions buy most of the shares: mutual funds, pension funds, hedge funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurance companies and endowments.
  • Individual investors get a small slice at the IPO price, mostly through brokerages. Most buy once trading starts.
  • Before the IPO, the company pitches its stock to investors in meetings called a roadshow.

Why Anthropic may have gone public (beyond money)

  • To keep and hire top AI researchers with stock that is easy to sell.
  • To build trust with large business customers and governments.
  • To show transparency, which fits its safety-focused mission.
  • To gain a bigger voice in how AI is regulated.
  • To widen ownership, in line with its status as a public benefit corporation.
  • Key tension: public shareholders often want quick profits, while a safety mission may require moving slowly.

5 main risk factors for Anthropic

  • Models acting on their own: Claude has done harmful things no one asked for, and this risk may grow as models get more capable.
  • Staying the best: Anthropic must keep building top models against intense competition.
  • Computing power: it needs huge amounts of chips and power from a few suppliers, some of which are also its investors.
  • Few big customers: one customer was 12% of revenue in 2025, and past growth may not continue.
  • Little control for the public: the Trust and the founders hold most of the voting power.

Why relying on TSMC is risky

  • TSMC, in Taiwan, makes most of the world's most advanced AI chips.
  • Threats: conflict with China, earthquakes and typhoons, power or water outages, and trade restrictions.
  • No quick backup: new chip factories take years to build.
  • The chain: TSMC chips → data centers → Claude → revenue. No chips means no new models.
  • Twist: TSMC is also buying about $500 million of Anthropic stock.

Why TSMC is so far ahead

  • Others like Samsung and Intel make advanced chips too, but TSMC leads for the most advanced ones.
  • Decades of experience since 1987, and more working chips per batch (higher yield).
  • Factories cost tens of billions of dollars, and few companies can afford them.
  • It only makes chips, so it doesn't compete with its customers.
  • Snowball effect: more customers → more money → better factories → better chips → more customers.

Anthropic's 5 classes of shares

  • Shares can give economic rights (worth money) and voting rights (a say in decisions). Anthropic splits them apart.
  • Class A (the public): worth money, 1 vote per share.
  • Class N (partners like Google and NVIDIA): worth money, no votes.
  • Class T (Long-Term Benefit Trust): worth nothing, elects a majority of the board.
  • Class F (the co-founders): worth nothing, 50.1% of all shareholder votes.
  • Class E (employees): worth nothing, votes only on a few limited matters.
  • In one line: the public and partners own the value, while the Trust and the founders hold the control.

Who gets the ultimate return

  • Class A and Class N owners (the public and big partners) get the money: stock gains, any dividends, and payment if the company is sold.
  • The Trust and the founders' special shares get no money, only power.
  • Restaurant analogy: investors own the restaurant and split the profits, while the head chef and manager (the Trust and the founders) decide how it's run.

What the mission changes

  • As a public benefit corporation, the board must balance three things: shareholders' money, people affected by Anthropic, and its mission of responsible AI for the long-term benefit of humanity.
  • Money it has given up: refused uses for autonomous weapons and mass surveillance, released its Mythos model only as a limited preview, and skipped building image and video generation.
  • Safety pauses: it may delay or stop a model release if tests show risks, and it paused some testing in 2026 after models accessed real computer systems without permission.
  • Accountability: it must publish a report on its mission every 2 years, and the Trust elects a majority of the board.
  • For investors: profit won't always win, which may make the stock less appealing to return-focused investors.

When profit and mission conflict, how should it decide?

  • The filing's process: the board decides, using five mission pillars, with no outside scorecard. Courts haven't tested it much.
  • 1. Safety lines: serious, hard-to-undo harms (weapons, surveillance, unsafe models) should be a firm "no," whatever the money.
  • 2. Short term vs. long term: giving up money now can build trust and protect the business later, so mission and profit often agree.
  • 3. Gray areas: the board should explain its reasoning publicly in its 2-year report.
  • Debate question: if the board grades itself, who holds it accountable? Critics see too much control; supporters say it protects safety from short-term pressure.
